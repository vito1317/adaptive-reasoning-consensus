Dear Editors,

\vspace{1em}

I am writing to submit my manuscript entitled \textbf{``TACT: Trust-Anchored
Confidence Tempering for Self-Consistency Voting in Large Language Models''}
for publication as a regular article in the \textit{Journal of Machine Learning
Research}.

The paper addresses a structural gap in confidence-weighted self-consistency:
every published weighting scheme is monotone \emph{increasing} in confidence,
so a channel whose confidence is anti-correlated with correctness poisons the
vote, and binary calibration gates survive that inversion only by discarding
discriminative signal. TACT derives the vote exponent from the measured,
\emph{signed}, within-item discrimination of the channel --- a pooled van
Elteren Somers' $D$ with an item-clustered standard error, positive-part
James--Stein shrinkage, and a Bayes-discriminant link --- so the exponent is
derived rather than grid-searched, and the construction has exact endpoints:
inside the shrinkage dead zone the vote is bit-identical to plain
self-consistency.

I should be direct with the editors about what the paper concludes, because it
is not a straightforward win. Alongside the method, the manuscript reports its
own negative measurements: a dev-picked \emph{signed} grid turns out to be a
far stronger baseline than any published protocol; the stratum on which
\emph{any} label-free aggregation method can act measures $2.5$--$7.5\%$ of
items across five substrates in two domains; on both real-trace substrates an
in-pool oracle cannot clear a pre-registered endpoint; and the label-free
arm's abstention on one substrate turns out to be structural rather than a
reading of the channel --- a defect found deliberately, by planting a channel
of known strength and watching the method fail to act on it. The negative
results are the contribution as much as the estimator is, and the paper is
submitted on that basis rather than as a claim of improved accuracy.
